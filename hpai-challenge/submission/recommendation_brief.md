# HPAI Epidemic Assessment and Policy Recommendations — Jolly Island

**Date**: 14 January 2026 | **Prepared by**: Sebastian Funk, LSHTM

---

## Epidemic status

As of 13 January 2026, 103 farms have been confirmed with HPAI over a 23-day period. The epidemic is concentrated in chicken farms (79% of cases) and in the southern high-risk zone, particularly Berks county (43% of cases). Broiler farms account for the majority of cases (72%). The epidemic curve is consistent with the beginning of a plateau, possibly reflecting waning wild-bird spillover pressure.

## Four-week forecast

Under current management (reactive culling, surveillance zones, preventive culling, pre-shipment testing), the model predicts **45 additional confirmed cases** (95% CrI: 22–79) over the next four weeks (14 Jan – 10 Feb 2026). The daily incidence is expected to remain at approximately 2 new confirmed cases per day, with a gradual decline as environmental pressure from wild birds wanes. New cases will remain concentrated in the high-risk zone.

## Species contribution

Chickens are approximately **4 times more susceptible** to HPAI than ducks (β_duck posterior median 0.24, 95% CrI: 0.13–0.37; implied chicken relative risk 4.2×, 95% CrI: 2.7–7.5×). Despite comprising ~60% of active farms, ducks contribute only ~20% of total susceptibility-weighted exposure. Chickens bear a disproportionate burden of infection.

## Policy evaluation

**Chicken-only preventive culling (Q4)**: Under the spillover-dominated model, which fits the observed data adequately, restricting preventive culling to chicken farms only has **negligible epidemiological impact** (predicted cases: 45 vs 45 baseline). Even if farm-to-farm transmission plays a role, the effect of excluding duck farms from preventive culling is modest given their low susceptibility.

**Faster reactive culling without preventive culling (Q5)**: Replacing preventive culling with a 1-day faster reactive culling also has **negligible impact under spillover-dominated dynamics** (predicted cases: 44 vs 45). Under a farm-to-farm transmission scenario, the benefit of faster removal is approximately offset by the loss of preventive culling, resulting in no net gain.

## Key recommendations

1. **Determine whether farm-to-farm transmission is occurring**: This is the most important short-term monitoring priority. Under spillover-dominated dynamics, culling policy changes have negligible effect; if spatial transmission is contributing, the policy conclusions change substantially. Systematic investigation of spatial case patterns — looking for clustering beyond what wild-bird pressure alone would explain — is recommended.

2. **Maintain current policy**: The combined reactive + preventive culling strategy is reasonable. Neither proposed modification offers meaningful improvement under the spillover-dominated model. The posterior decay rate (δ = 0.011/day, half-life ~63 days) implies spillover pressure will persist through February, declining only ~27% over the next four weeks.

3. **Focus resources on the high-risk zone**: The spillover hazard in the HRZ is approximately 7× higher than outside. Surveillance and response capacity should remain concentrated there.

4. **Consider species-targeted surveillance**: Given the ~4× higher susceptibility in chickens, enhanced monitoring of chicken broiler operations in the HRZ would improve early detection.
